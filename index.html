What ageism is

The World Health Organization defines it in three parts: stereotypes (how we think), prejudice (how we feel), and discrimination (how we act) — toward others or oneself, based on age. The term was coined in 1969 by Robert Butler, building on the language of sexism and racism. It lives at three levels:

Institutional

Policies and practices that perpetuate stereotypes: healthcare rationed by age, hiring filtered by birth year, services denied by category. The rules themselves discriminate.

Interpersonal

How people treat each other: patronizing speech, dismissed ideas, opportunities closed because someone is “too young” or “too old.” Children as young as four already absorb their culture’s age stereotypes.

Self-directed

What people internalize about their own age — self-limiting beliefs about what someone of a given age can be or do. Older people themselves can carry it, after a lifetime of hearing it.

A note on language, which this project models: the WHO deliberately uses neutral terms — older people, not pejorative labels. Words carry the stereotypes they describe. This site chooses its words accordingly.

The last socially acceptable prejudice

Most organizations now have departments for racism and gender bias. Age bias is seldom on their radar — researchers call it the odd “-ism” in that it remains socially acceptable in many ways, from birthday cards that joke about getting older to memes that dismiss whole generations. The American Psychological Association adopted a resolution on ageism in 2020, recognizing age as a risk factor for discrimination. But the stereotypes underneath it are often simply false:

Aging is not all decline

Most older adults maintain good health and cognitive functioning. Some abilities slow; others hold or improve — older adults orient attention and ignore distractions better than midlife adults, and creativity can grow more generative later in life.

The paradox of aging

Older people report greater happiness and life satisfaction than people in midlife — a U-shaped curve confirmed across 145 countries. People overestimate the problems of later life and underestimate its resilience.

This is the new concept of aging the APA cover story describes: not rose-colored lenses, but rigorous science — aging as a transition with strengths, not a synonym for decline. The prejudice survives because the true story isn’t told often enough.

Why AI makes it worldwide

Ageism was already global. AI makes it exportable. A hiring tool trained on ageist data doesn’t stay in one country — it ships everywhere. A triage model, a credit model, a feed algorithm: each one carries the stereotypes it learned, applies them invisibly, and scales them to millions of decisions a day.

That is also why the old prejudice is newly hard to challenge. The WHO’s description of ageism — prevalent, ubiquitous, and insidious because it goes largely unrecognized and unchallenged — now describes its automated form exactly. A biased human can be argued with. A biased system just returns a score.

And it cuts both ways in the digital world: older people depicted as digitally inept, their practices excluded from research, their faces underrepresented or misrepresented in media — digital ageism and visual ageism, now amplified by the systems that generate what everyone sees.

What Ageism.ai does

Document

A global case log of age bias in AI systems and ageist institutions, country by country — every case sourced, every claim checkable. The record nobody else is keeping.

Measure

An annual index ranking countries and systems on AI age bias and on protections against it — published methodology, comparable year to year, built to be cited.

Mobilize

A campaign in the frame of the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing, which runs through 2030: education, intergenerational contact, and policy — the three strategies the WHO finds actually work.

Go-forward plan

Worldwide is a claim to be earned, not a banner to be hung. The plan sequences the work so each phase funds the next one’s credibility:

Phase 1

Document — the global case log

Build the log first: verified cases of age bias in AI systems and ageist policy, organized by country, every case carrying its source. Start with English-language reporting and add regional sources deliberately — a worldwide log with one country’s stories is a worldwide claim waiting to collapse. No case without a source, ever.

Phase 2

Measure — the Ageism.ai Index

Publish the first annual index: countries ranked on documented AI age bias and on the strength of their protections, with the methodology published alongside the ranking so it can be challenged and improved. An index that can’t be argued with isn’t an index — it’s a press release.

Phase 3

Mobilize — campaign and partners

Take the evidence to the frame that already exists: the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing, through 2030. Regional partners, multilingual reach, and programs built on what works — education that dispels misconceptions, and intergenerational contact that is prepared, not incidental. Policy follows evidence; the log and the index are the evidence.

Three principles govern the whole plan. Evidence first: nothing is published without a source. Global means global: coverage earns the claim, branding doesn’t. Mission before brand: a worldwide banner with a thin mission reads as exploitation — so the mission stays thicker than the marketing.
